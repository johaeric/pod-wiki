# Brain Training (BrainHQ-style)

Computerised cognitive-training programmes — most often attention and processing-speed games — built on neuroplasticity principles. Distinct from [[cognitive-rehab]] (clinician-led compensatory strategies) and from "brain games" of the casual-puzzle variety.

## Anchor

[[090-dr-james-jackson-brain-fog]] (Vanderbilt neuropsychologist) names **BrainHQ** as a specific platform with growing evidence base in attention and processing speed. Frames it as **complementary** to cognitive rehab rather than a substitute.

## Where it sits relative to cognitive rehab

| | Cognitive rehab | Brain training |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | Specific functional deficit (memory / EF / processing speed) | Underlying capacity (esp. attention, speed) |
| **Delivery** | Clinician-led (SLP / OT) | Self-directed at home, computerised |
| **Strategy** | Compensatory + external scaffolding | Repeated drilling on calibrated tasks |
| **Cost** | Typically insurance / NHS where accessible | Subscription (BrainHQ ~$100/year) |

For LC, Jackson's pragmatic stance: brain training is worth trying if cognitive rehab is unavailable or as adjunct.

## Caveats

- **PEM exposure**: like cognitive rehab, brain training is cognitive load. Pace it.
- The "brain games" controversy is mostly about generic puzzle apps with no calibration; the evidence stronger platforms cite (BrainHQ, Cogmed) is specifically about adaptive-difficulty processing-speed and attention training.
- Not a magic pill. Effects are measurable but modest in healthy populations; LC-specific evidence is preliminary.
