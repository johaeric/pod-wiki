# #105 — Calum Carson on the Inclusive Remote & Hybrid Working Study

Senior research associate at Lancaster University, working on the **Inclusive Remote and Hybrid Working Study** (PI Dr Paula Holland, Lancaster). The study explores **disabled workers' experiences of remote and hybrid working over the past five years** with the explicit goal of preventing new inequalities from getting embedded into post-pandemic working norms before they have time to.

Carson himself has **degenerative disc disease** (back pain) since his PhD years (2017–20); his own arc — pandemic-onset home-working as the thing that kept him in academia — informs the project.

## The study (Lancaster + partners)

- **PI**: Dr Paula Holland (Lancaster; ran the smaller predecessor project)
- **Partners**: The Work Foundation (in-house think tank), Manchester Metropolitan University, Universal Inclusion (charity)
- **Funder**: not specified in episode, but academic-grant funded
- **Phase 1 (current)**: UK-wide online survey, target **~1,500 participants**, 759 at recording. Open to anyone in the UK who self-identifies as **disabled and/or has a long-term physical or mental health condition** AND has experienced remote / hybrid working in the past 5 years (need not be currently working).
- **Phase 2**: ~50 follow-up interviews with survey respondents who indicated willingness
- **Phase 3**: employer case studies (HR managers; sector-specific challenges)
- **Phase 4**: policy-maker engagement (local + national; current guidance / case law / Equality Act questions)
- **Phase 5 (Dec 2024 launch)**: report (publicly accessible, non-academic language) + **commissioned short film** featuring 4–5 interviewees telling their stories visually, in their own homes
- Plus a **best-practice guide for employers** drawn from positive examples surfaced in the data

## Operational principle: self-defined disability

Deliberately permissive scope. Examples Carson gives of conditions Holland's team wants represented:

- Long-term mental health (depression, anxiety)
- Neurodivergence (autism, ADHD)
- Cancer recovery in-work
- Long Covid (no legal definition yet — explicitly included)
- Fluctuating conditions (Carson's framing throughout)

**The frame**: if it's affecting your working life long-term, your voice is wanted. Legal disability status is irrelevant for the survey. Cuts past the "is LC technically a disability?" gate that gatekeeps a lot of LC employment research.

## Anchor concern: new inequalities in disguise

The risk Carson articulates clearest:

> "Employers say 'you've now got more flexibility to work from home, so we don't need to provide as many reasonable adjustments in the office'… and that takes the freedom away from people to *want* to come in the office, to go to Thursday drinks."

Home-working as **substitution** for in-office reasonable adjustments rather than as **supplement** would mean disabled workers lose the autonomy to choose — and risk being structurally pushed out of office-side promotion / social capital networks.

Compounded by the **binary public framing** in mainstream press ("forced office return" vs "fully remote") that Carson critiques. The actual disabled-worker ask is *flexibility within fluctuating capacity*, not residence on either pole.

## Useful framings for LC employment context

Carson articulates several things the wiki's [[employment]] theme should pick up:

- **Education + healthcare are the LC-overrepresented sectors** (Jackie's observation; Carson confirms). They're also among the **least adaptable** to remote working — a structural double-burden the study is positioned to surface.
- **Manager-level flexibility matters more than organisational policy.** A "remote strategy" that mandates Mondays and Fridays in the office defeats its own flexibility claim. Carson's PhD on the living wage found the same pattern — formal benefits matter less than a flexible day-to-day manager who lets you collect a sick child from school.
- **Promotion-pathway anxiety**: disabled workers in the predecessor study explicitly worried that *not* coming in would cost them promotion access. The "social capital" of office life is itself a workplace asset that flexible-working policies don't yet account for.
- **Cross-beneficiary case**: hybrid-working accommodations also benefit single parents, carers, and others who aren't (or don't identify as) disabled. Frame for political coalition-building beyond the disability constituency.

## Why this study matters on the wiki

- **Window-of-opportunity research.** Post-pandemic working norms are forming *now*. The study's goal is to surface inequalities before they harden — five-year-late research can document harm but can't prevent it.
- **Outputs designed for non-academic audiences** — film, accessible report, employer best-practice guide. Treats the people whose voices it's collecting as also the audience.
- **Open to LC participation.** Eric / anyone reading with LC who has done remote/hybrid work in the past 5 years can contribute — this is one of the few research pieces where a survey response materially shapes downstream policy work.

## Eric-relevance

- **Direct invitation to participate** in the survey (UK-only at this stage). Eric's remote/hybrid arc through severe LC is exactly the kind of data Holland's team is trying to capture.
- **Sectoral data** — when published, the report will likely have specific findings on healthcare and education sectors (the LC-overrepresented ones), of cross-condition relevance.
- **Best-practice guide** will be operationally usable for any LC patient negotiating with an employer about reasonable adjustments — worth tracking the late-2024 launch.
- The **"new inequalities in disguise"** framing is worth Eric having vocabulary for if/when his own employer offers home-working as a *substitute* for, rather than supplement to, other reasonable adjustments.
