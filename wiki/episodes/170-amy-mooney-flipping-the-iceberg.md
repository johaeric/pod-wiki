# #170 — Amy Mooney: Flipping the Iceberg

US occupational therapist (~25 years, paediatric / sensory-integration background). For the last ~10 years she has been the primary caregiver for her now-19-year-old daughter, who first became unwell at age 9 with what was eventually diagnosed as ME/CFS. That decade of caregiver-side lived experience — combined with her clinical background — has reshaped her practice toward chronic-illness-aware OT for ME/CFS / LC. Affiliated with [[bateman-horne-center]] for outreach and education.

## The iceberg framing

- Conventional rehab (PT, OT, including her own initial work with her daughter) sees only the **tip** — what the patient can perform in the clinic.
- Beneath the surface: the cost paid in PEM, the days-to-weeks of crash that follow.
- The *teacher metaphor*: "she was like a duck — looked still on the surface but paddling like crazy underneath; she could perform but couldn't keep up; the iceberg was pulling her under."
- **The therapy needs to happen on the flipped iceberg** — between the activities, in the rest, in the symptom-management, not in performance.

## Aggressive rest as the load-bearing intervention

- **Aggressive rest** ≠ doing nothing. It is *deliberate sensory + cognitive + somatic deactivation* during rest periods.
  - Cut visual input (eye masks)
  - Cut sound input (ear plugs / noise cancelling)
  - Quiet internal buzzing (vibration tools like **Sensate**, weighted blanket pressure, joint compressions / pedalling-with-legs proprioceptive input *without* exertion)
  - Temperature regulation
- The body needs *something* to register safety; it can be hard to get to deep rest from a wired-tired state without input that satisfies the nervous system.
- **The hardest practice on earth.** Most patients have been culturally trained that rest = laziness. Mooney spends significant practitioner time *reframing* aggressive rest as the active treatment.
- Good rest → baseline rises slowly → window for prioritised activity widens.

## Two types of rest (Bruce Campbell lineage)

- **Pre-emptive rest**: planning *before* an activity. Lower the relevant exertion buckets in advance. E.g. before a shower: lie down with feet elevated; ice pack at base of neck for autonomic calming.
- **Recuperative rest**: planning *after*. Pre-arranged "support kit" — robe ready, bed pre-warmed, electrolytes ready, vibration tool, smoothie. Decisions made *before* the activity, not while crashing.

## The exertion-buckets model

Each activity draws from one or more of:
- **Physical** (steps, standing time)
- **Cognitive** (reading, screens, conversation)
- **Sensory** (light, sound, motion — Mooney calls sensory the *umbrella* over all)
- **Social-emotional** (happy *and* sad — euphoria is metabolically costly too)

Each bucket has its own current capacity (water-line). Patients need to know *which bucket* an activity loads more, and which symptom each over-load triggers most for them. After a shower (physical-up): next activity should *not* be physical — choose from a different bucket.

## What is occupation, really

- "Occupation" in OT vocabulary = your daily-life roles (sister, partner, gardener, caregiver, friend), not just "your job."
- ME/CFS / LC strips occupations away from the *outside in*: hobbies → social roles → work → self-care.
- The therapeutic question is **what's most meaningful to *this* patient?** — not what's expected. Mooney's example: a patient who chose to spend her best 20 minutes *writing an email to her grandkids* over taking a shower. That's the right OT priority.

## Increasing capacity gently — Mooney's signal that you're ready

- The recuperative-rest *window shortens*. Where a shower used to need 3 days of crash recovery, now 2 days, less intense.
- That shortening is the cue that you can introduce the next small activity — but in *a different exertion bucket* (not stack physical-on-physical).
- **Three-day delayed PEM** is real — even if today felt fine, day 3 may bring it. After any out-of-baseline activity, give it the 3-day window before assuming you can do more.

## Eric-relevant takeaways

- **Aggressive rest is operationally distinct from Eric's current rest practice.** Worth experimenting with: eye mask + earplugs + weighted blanket / vibration tool in a rest session; deliberate sensory deactivation rather than "lying down with audiobook."
- **Pre-emptive rest before high-load activity** (long meetings, social events, travel) — Eric already paces; adding the *pre-emptive lie-down + ice-pack-on-neck* could be a contained tactical addition.
- **Recuperative-rest support kit** is a discrete project Eric can set up once and re-use: pre-warmed bed, electrolytes ready, vibration tool, eye mask, smoothie ingredients in fridge — *decisions pre-made*, so a crash doesn't require executive function.
- **Exertion buckets** is a useful lens: not all of Eric's PEM is physical. Cognitive load (work) and social-emotional load (anything difficult) draw from different buckets that can fill faster than the physical one.
- **3-day window before declaring an activity "safe"** is operationally important — Eric should resist the temptation to scale up after a "fine" first day.
