# #78 — Lucy Gahan on the Psychology of Long Covid

Clinical psychologist who got covid in April 2020. Off work for **19 months**; returned 6 months ago into an NHS long-covid service ("where I belong for now"). Author of **Reclaiming Life and the Things That Matter** — a short, accessible book heavily cited by the wiki for its psychological framing.

## Her clinical picture

- April 2020 covid. "Moderate" acute illness with severe breathing difficulty.
- Couple of weeks later: standing in kitchen → near-faint → POTS-like presentation.
- Months later: oxygen-desaturation on uprightness, frightening heart-rhythm episodes.
- Off work 19 months; returned 6 months pre-recording.

## Six concepts she lifts up from psychology

### 1. Trauma is repeated, not one-event

> "Long covid trauma isn't one thing that happens to you — it's something that happens to you over and over again."

Months of "wondering if I'll wake up tomorrow" as ongoing, layered trauma. Similar to combat-zone PTSD framing, not single-event PTSD.

### 2. Witnessing (Kaethe Weingarten)

To be **witnessed** is to be acknowledged in your experience by another person who registers it. To be **un-witnessed** — repeatedly dismissed, "looks fine," told it's anxiety — is itself an ongoing injury. Long covid patients are **systematically un-witnessed** by the medical system.

> "Anything that resonates is so powerful — having that feeling acknowledged matters enormously."

This is *why* recovery stories, podcasts, and books like Gahan's matter: each act of resonance is a witnessing.

### 3. Self-loss

The illness takes away most of what made you you — exercise, work, social roles, identity. Patients describe "I don't recognise myself half the time."

### 4. Chronic sorrow (vs depression)

A more accurate label than depression for the recurring grief that surfaces at each new loss/limit. One of Gahan's clients described the relief of having **chronic sorrow** named — "this fits, I thought I was just depressed."

### 5. Progressive vs non-progressive illness narratives (Weingarten)

A cold has a known shape — 10 days, then better. People know how to talk to you. **Long covid has no known arc**, no diagnosis, no agreed treatment, no agreed prognosis → much harder to talk about, much harder to receive empathy. Even the way you tell the story has no scaffold.

### 6. Layers of difficulty

Not "the symptoms" alone but: symptoms → losses (work, exercise, identity, relationships) → not-being-witnessed/dismissed by medics → forgiveness / re-attachment work as recovery progresses. Each layer needs its own attention.

## "Lingering" — language critique

Gahan strongly objects to "lingering effects of covid" in media coverage:

> "Lingering says some kind of mild post-viral effect that goes away on its own. It misses the cascade. And lingering effects don't justify a service — so funding follows the language."

Naming matters; "post-viral" and "lingering" are softer-than-reality terms that distort funding and public understanding.

## On the patterns of PEM and "downtime"

She makes a specific point that **emotional energy, conversations, arguments, even reading** all use energy — not just physical activity. Her client said *"You're using up my oxygen"* mid-argument.

> "I sat at the kitchen table the day after a quiet day at work, exhausted. *How does that work?* I know how it works. We have to keep learning the same lesson."

## The yoga mat as psychological space

Yoga, "lazy" practice from her mat, became her exercise. **The 20 min on the mat is a psychological space** as much as physical: rolling out the mat = appointment with self → notice today's body → rest *or* gentle work. She names this an "island in the day."

## What she did that helped

- Connecting with **Noah Greenspan** / Pulmonary Wellness Foundation videos on dysautonomia → the moment she realised it was real and had a name.
- Joining LC Facebook groups (Long Covid Fighters) — connection.
- Yoga — exercise + space + identity-rebuilding.
- Writing the book (started as 100-day journal entries).
