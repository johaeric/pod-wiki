Nadine McKie — recovery-focused psychotherapist now working at Dr Bonita Kane's Long COVID clinic. Recovered LC patient herself, and her son also recovered from LC. Returns for a focused conversation on **carers and co-regulation**.

## The carer's invisible load

- Caring for an LC patient mirrors LC itself — invisible, exhausting, isolating
- Relational rather than protocol-based — there is no welltrodden treatment path, so families bear the weight
- Different carer relationships: parent-child, parent-adult-child returning home, partner-partner — each has distinct dynamics

## Where the carer's role ends

- Support and listening = the carer's job
- Telling the patient what to do / fixing = *not* the carer's job
- Patients should learn to *understand themselves better* (empowering) rather than *take responsibility for fixing themselves* (shaming)
- "You can't be everything to another person — including the person you love and care for"

## Why the carer's nervous system matters

- Best regulator of a human nervous system is *another* regulated human nervous system — and the worst dysregulator is also another nervous system
- A calm carer presence reduces threat signals → patient's nervous system can downregulate → recovery is more possible
- Disregulated carer ↔ disregulated patient ↔ further disregulation feedback loop

## Practical for carers

- Get your own external support (friend / therapist / community)
- Recognise emotional load — anger, frustration, grief, resentment are valid; create a place to express them *away* from the patient
- Take micro-breaks; small rests count
- Don't add a self-care to-do list to your already-burdened list — be choosy about what works
- Outsource where possible (cleaning, food, friend-help)

## What people with LC can do for carers

- Recognise that having needs is OK
- Learn to communicate them clearly — and also recognise the carer has needs
- Maintain empathy and grace in both directions

## Vicarious trauma

- Long-term advocacy + dismissal from systems + uncertainty + isolation can create vicarious trauma in carers
- Carers need their own recovery work

## Notes for Eric

The most actionable takeaway: **the regulating presence of one human is the most powerful nervous-system tool for another human**. Worth holding from both sides — Eric's own regulation supports those around him; the regulation of those around him supports his recovery. The "stop trying to fix the patient" admonition is a good gentle reminder for any partner / carer / clinician relationship.

## Key quote

> "The best thing for our nervous system is another person. And the worst thing for our nervous system is another person. So how the carer regulates — that's part of the patient's treatment."
> — Nadine McKie, ep #205
