# #100 — Michelle Irving on the four emotional stages of chronic illness

Australian coach (autoimmune liver disease + vertigo of her own — not LC) bringing **The Emotional Empowerment Map for Chronic Illness** into the podcast for the first time. Not a clinical episode, no interventions named — instead a single re-usable framework that organises the inner work patients are doing across all the recovery stories.

## The four stages

A *psychological* map, deliberately decoupled from physical recovery. One blood test, one symptom, can move you between stages in either direction.

1. **At sea (patient in crisis)** — drifting, can't see land, often pre-diagnosis. Job: *be at sea and take care of yourself while you're at sea*. Question: **"what helps?"** — at the bedside, with food, with helpers.
2. **Washed up on the beach** — small capacity returns, helpers disappear, **the emotional backlog washes over you** (grief especially). Job: learn limits without converting limit-testing into "I've ruined it." Compares to a toddler walking — fall, get up, no narrative of failure.
3. **Making your way through the forest** — more capacity; small autonomies (going to the market and choosing your own oranges) are emotionally nourishing. Pace gathers.
4. **Sharing the wisdom / return to the village** — re-integration. Counter-intuitive: identity itself can feel "at sea" again because the person who returns is not the person who left.

## The vertical/horizontal life model (the closing image)

> "Normal life is horizontal — school, uni, work, relationship. Illness sends you vertical — *down* into yourself. Identity, status, ways-of-feeling-power get stripped. You shoot back up further along the horizontal — looks like you went back to work, but psychologically you've gone a thousand kilometres."
> — Michelle Irving, ep #100

This is the wiki's clearest articulation of why returning-to-life patients (Jackie #94, Charlotte #98) report life feeling "weirder than expected" — they're not the same person re-entering the same horizontal track.

## Operational principles

- **"What helps?" is the foundational question** at every stage — practiced at-sea, then portable forward.
- **Helpful vs unhelpful helpers** — unhelpful = unsolicited advice / articles / "someone I knew who…". Fix: give them concrete jobs ("could you wash the bed linen on Sunday so I start the week clean?"). Don't fire them, *task them*.
- **Asking for help as skill, not failure** — "I had failed as a human if I had to ask for help" was her shame-script; being-helped is itself the work, not the shameful detour around the work.
- **Pity party as legitimate** — "I'm 100% for a really good pity party. Done well, mine lasts about three days and I'm over myself."
- **Grief is normal; hopelessness is grief gone stuck.** Re-route via "what helps me feel a little more connected" — connection is the antidote to the loneliness underneath hopelessness.
- **Limits are skills you didn't have before.** Pre-illness most people don't know their energy boundaries; illness teaches them. Post-recovery, that knowledge stays.

## Reframe of the "illness was a gift" controversy

Jackie pushes back on the trope ("three and a half years of my life I didn't get to live"). Michelle's reframe: not *illness as gift* but **illness as doorway into your own sovereignty** — the person who comes through has skills (vulnerability tolerance, emotional honesty, choice-awareness) that most people only acquire in their 50s if at all. The wiki's most useable form of this difficult debate.

## Eric-relevance

The framework is portable across any chronic-illness presentation — POTS/PEM included.
