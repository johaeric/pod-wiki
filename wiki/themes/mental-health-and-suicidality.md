# Mental health & suicidality in Long Covid

The mental-health dimension of LC is **not optional** — it is a load-bearing clinical concern that the wiki tracks alongside the physical mechanism stack. Two episodes have so far named explicit treatment of LC-driven mood disturbance with antidepressant medication; the most consequential is [[saskia-mulder]] #48, who reached active suicidality with a developing plan at 16–17 months and credits an SSRI with stopping the ideation in days.

## What we have learned from the podcast so far

- **LC drives genuine, severe depression and suicidality** — separate from any pre-existing mental health history. The mechanism stack (autonomic chronic alarm, inflammation, sleep disruption, cognitive impairment, identity loss, financial stress, social isolation, healthcare gaslighting) all converge.
- **Recognising the warning signs is critical.** Mulder, herself a psychiatric nurse, recognised that her sense of *relief* from having a developed plan was itself a danger sign — she knew from clinical experience that suicidal patients who suddenly seem brighter have often "made the decision."
- **Healthcare workers may delay disclosure longer.** Both Twycross (#42) and Mulder (#48) describe nurse-specific friction — the trained-helper-doesn't-want-to-be-the-patient pattern. Twycross mentions colleagues at Long Covid Nurses & Midwives UK with shared experience.
- **The stigma worry can be lethal.** Mulder's hesitation — "I didn't want to give the doctors the pleasure of saying 'didn't we tell you it was in your head'" — almost cost her her life. The wiki holds firmly: in LC, starting an antidepressant for active suicidality is not concession to gaslighting.
- **SSRIs work fast for this specific use case.** Mulder reports ideation stopping within days. This is consistent with what you'd expect for crisis-driven (rather than chronic-baseline) depression. Twycross reports similar fast benefit on sleep and affect.

## Practical heuristic

If you (or someone you support) with LC:

- Has lost the ability to imagine a viable future
- Is finding *comfort or relief* in a death plan
- Is researching specific methods or accumulating means
- Has stopped maintaining basic self-care
- Has withdrawn from previously sustaining contacts
- Has an unusually calm or "lifted" mood after a long depression

**Treat as urgent, not as a phase to wait out.** Disclose to a trusted person. Contact the GP / OOH service / 111 / 999 / Samaritans (UK 116 123) / international crisis line. Do not wait for a "better time."

## Convergent gaslighting context

LC patients in 2020-2022 routinely faced healthcare gaslighting — the "it's in your head" framing — that made disclosure of mental-health symptoms feel like *vindicating the gaslighters*. This is exactly the trap Mulder fell into. The right reframe: treating depression in LC is treating a downstream symptom of a real physical illness, not conceding the illness is psychological.

## Two further patterns added 2026-05-05 (#183, #186)

**The 80%+ "mental health is the gating factor" pattern (Theresa #183).** Theresa Aristoark made significant physical-therapy progress for ~6 months, quit her job, then crashed hard with new vestibular symptoms and a panic attack 8 weeks before her wedding. Her partner Jake — who'd lived with her through the whole illness — sat her down and told her the unaddressed *mental health* layer was now the blocker. SSRI started → "stepping into sunlight" within weeks; physical strength returned in parallel; got married, honeymooned, hiked 7 miles a year later. **For LC patients who've done significant physical work and plateaued, an honest mental-health audit (with a partner who'll tell the truth) may be the missing piece.** Adds to the Mulder/Twycross pattern: SSRI in LC is not always a crisis intervention — sometimes it's the unblocking move at high recovery percentage.

**The bedbound mental-crisis pattern (Jamie #186).** Jamie reached active suicidality (asking for knives) while severely bedbound, after a high-supplement detox protocol had reframed all her adverse reactions as "die-off." She was hospitalised; given olanzapine for the acute crisis; that stabilised her sleep and overall affect enough that she could engage with brain retraining. **Olanzapine is NOT a treatment for LC** — it is an emergency psychiatric medication, hard to taper, and Jamie was still tapering at recording. But the pattern matters: sometimes the path forward requires acute psychiatric stabilisation before recovery work is even possible. Don't skip a hospital admission for fear of the LC label.
